US AI policy gets enacted where it is easiest to undo

Tobyn Smith, tobynsmith@uga.edu, University of Georgia

Research question and hypothesis

Does who issues an AI policy affect whether it gets enacted?

H1: AI policies issued by federal agencies are more likely to be enacted than AI bills introduced in Congress.

H0: The issuing authority makes no difference to whether an AI policy is enacted.

Literature Review and Theory

It is evident that legislative gridlocks often limit what Congress enacts (Binder, 1999). At the same time, while federal agencies can issue rules without congressional approval, later administrations can easily revoke them.

During the literature review, Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024) established that judges establish what unclear laws mean, making agency rules easier to challenge.

This is reinforced by four agency AI documents in the AGORA dataset were classified as defunct following policy decisions by the current administration.

Gap: The goal of this project is to establish this literature gap as it looks at whether the issuer of an AI policy changes how likely it is to be enacted.

Key finding

Assuming Congress and agency AI documents are comparable (a strong assumption, because the issuers cover different topics), I estimate that issuance by a federal agency increases the share enacted by 36.7 percentage points on average.

Policy implications

When a new administration enters office, they can undo agency AI rules. As a result, core agency AI rules should be placed into statute. The agencies should also outline which AI rules are more vulnerable to reversals if those reversals could hamper agency effectiveness.

Limitations and next steps

  • Issuers were not randomly assigned.
  • Some of AGORA’s policy summaries contain machine-generated text that has not been reviewed, so they would need fact-checking.

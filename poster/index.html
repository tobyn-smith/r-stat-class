INTL 7007 · University of Georgia

US AI policy gets enacted where it is easiest to undo

Tobyn Smith, tobynsmith@uga.edu

Research question

Does who issues an AI policy affect whether it gets enacted?

H1. AI policies issued by federal agencies are more likely to be enacted than AI bills introduced in Congress.

H0. The issuing authority makes no difference to whether an AI policy is enacted.

Why it matters

Congress often deadlocks (Binder, 1999). Agency rules skip Congress, and a new administration can undo them.

Since Loper Bright (2024), courts no longer defer to agencies on the law.

In 2025, four agency AI rules here went Defunct, including the 2023 AI executive order.

  • Executive Order on the Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence (Executive Office of the President), 2023-10-30 to 2025-01-20.
  • Advancing Governance, Innovation, and Risk Management for Agency Use of Artificial Intelligence (OMB), 2024-03-28 to 2025-04-03.
  • Advancing the Responsible Acquisition of Artificial Intelligence in Government (OMB), 2024-10-03 to 2025-04-03.
  • Framework for Artificial Intelligence Diffusion (Department of Commerce), 2025-01-13 to 2025-05-13.

Data

AGORA v1.32.0, Emerging Technology Observatory. One row is one AGORA document. Big laws are split by AI section.

US federal and state, 2017 to 2026. I keep Congress, federal agencies and states (1,126 of 1,251 rows), then drop Defunct. N = 706.

Outcome: enacted (1) or proposed (0). Treatment: federal agency (53) versus Congress (364, from 177 bills). States (289) are context only. No extra controls in the main comparison.

How the sample was built: 1,251 documents → 125 other authorities dropped → 420 Defunct dropped (Congress 371, agencies 4, states 45) → 706 left.

Method

Difference in proportions (agency minus Congress) with a 95% CI and a z-test. Same slope from lm(enacted ~ agency).

Federal agencies: 48 of 53 enacted = 90.57%.

Congress: 196 of 364 enacted = 53.85%.

Difference: 90.57 − 53.85 = 36.72 points. 95% CI 27.26 to 46.18. z = 7.61; p < 0.001.

Agency AI policy is enacted far more often than Congress bills, and is the easiest to undo.

Checks

Defunct counted as not enacted: +57.5 (CI 47.5 to 67.6).

Without the Executive Office of the President: +37.6 (CI 26.9 to 48.3).

Validated rows only: −3.2 (CI −10.9 to 4.4; p = 0.41). This check is weak. 159 of 160 validated Congress rows are already enacted.

Without NDAA section rows: Congress enacted share falls to 22.2%, and the gap is +68.3 (CI 58.6 to 78.0). One law can add many enacted Congress rows.

Holding constant whether the policy mainly applies to government, the agency coefficient in lm() is still 0.49.

Text analysis

The printed poster only had room for a short note. The chart below uses AGORA short summaries. I drop stop words, months, drafting words and US state names. Each issuer is one bag of words. For words used by both issuers I correlate the word shares with cor() and cor.test().

On these charts the word-share correlation with Congress is 0.43 for agencies and 0.46 for states. In analysis.R I get r = 0.42 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.49, p < 0.001) for agencies and r = 0.46 (95% CI 0.40 to 0.51, p < 0.001) for states. The issuers are not writing about the same things. That makes the comparable-groups assumption shaky.

How alike is the language?

Each dot is a word used by both issuers. Words far from the dashed line lean to one side.

Interpretation

Assuming Congress and agency AI documents are comparable (a reasonable assumption because both come from one tracker over overlapping years), we estimate that issuance by a federal agency increases the share enacted by 36.7 percentage points on average.

I would not lean on that assumption. The issuers cover different topics, and validated rows show no gap.

Policy implications

That leaves US AI governance on fragile ground.

Congress should put core agency AI rules into statute. Trackers should flag rules open to reversal.

Limitations

Issuers were not randomly assigned.

Congress rows are bill sections, which are not independent.

318 of 706 summaries may include unreviewed machine output.

The agency group is small (n = 53).

Next: one row per bill, and clustered standard errors.
